# Open Problems

This file covers significant unsolved problems in ML/AI **as of November 2026** — framed honestly as open research problems, not issues on the verge of being solved. Unlike [near-term-outlook.md](near-term-outlook.md) and [long-term-outlook.md](long-term-outlook.md), this file makes few forward-looking predictions about *when* or *whether* these get solved, and focuses instead on precisely stating what's actually unresolved and why it's hard. Each problem now carries a short **Status (2025-26)** note summarizing what changed over the past year or so; none of those changes amounts to a solution.

## Catastrophic forgetting

**The problem.** When a trained model is further trained on new data (whether via full fine-tuning or continued pretraining), it tends to lose some of its previously-learned capabilities in the process — the new training signal can overwrite representations that were important for earlier-learned tasks/knowledge, since nothing in standard gradient-based training inherently protects previously-useful weight configurations.

**Why it's hard.** There's a fundamental tension here: a model needs to be plastic enough to actually learn new things, but stable enough not to lose old things — and standard neural network training doesn't have a principled, general-purpose way to have both simultaneously. This is the central obstacle to the continual/online learning direction discussed as a `[Projection]` in [long-term-outlook.md](long-term-outlook.md) — until this is better addressed, continuously updating a deployed model's weights from live data remains risky rather than routine practice.

**Status (2025-26).** New research directions, still early: Nested Learning and its Hope architecture (Google, NeurIPS 2025) give different parts of a model different update frequencies — a "continuum memory" from fast to slow — with continual-learning results reported only at small scale (roughly 760M-1.3B parameters); test-time training methods such as In-Place Test-Time Training (ICLR 2026) update a designated subset of fast weights during inference. A related symptom shows up in ordinary post-training: training one model on many RL objectives at once can make capabilities interfere with each other, and the widely reported 2026 practice of training separate specialist models and then combining them via on-policy distillation (see [near-term-outlook.md](near-term-outlook.md)) is partly a workaround for exactly that. The core problem — learning continually at frontier scale without forgetting — remains open.

## Hallucination and factuality

**The problem.** Language models, including capable frontier ones, can generate fluent, confident-sounding statements that are factually incorrect — a failure mode broadly called "hallucination." This isn't a simple bug to patch; it's a fairly direct consequence of how these models are trained (next-token prediction over training data — see [autoregressive-generation.md](../04-generative-models/autoregressive-generation.md) — optimizes for plausible-sounding continuations, which is correlated with, but not identical to, factual accuracy) and how they're evaluated (a model that confidently guesses often scores better on many benchmarks than one that appropriately expresses uncertainty, unless the evaluation is specifically designed to penalize confident wrongness).

**Why it's hard.** A model has no built-in, reliable mechanism for distinguishing "things I actually know well" from "things I'm pattern-matching a plausible-sounding answer for" — its internal confidence (as reflected in output probabilities) doesn't always correlate cleanly with actual correctness (a calibration problem — see the glossary). Mitigations exist (retrieval-augmented approaches that ground answers in retrieved source documents, RLHF/training approaches that reward appropriate expressions of uncertainty, verifiable-reward RL for domains where correctness is checkable — see [rl-for-llms.md](../07-reinforcement-learning/rl-for-llms.md)) but none of them constitutes a full solution, and this remains one of the most practically consequential open problems in deploying these systems for tasks where factual reliability matters.

**Status (2025-26).** Verifiable-reward RL spread widely after DeepSeek-R1 (January 2025), which helps precisely where an automatic checker exists (math, code, some tool outputs). It does not help in the same way for open-ended factual claims, where no cheap checker exists — so the gap between "checkable" and "uncheckable" domains is, if anything, more visible now. No general solution is known as of November 2026.

## Interpretability

**The problem.** For a large neural network, it remains generally difficult to give a precise, reliable, human-understandable account of *why* the model produced a specific output, or what internal computation led to a given behavior — the model's reasoning is distributed across billions of parameters in a way that resists simple explanation, in sharp contrast to something like a decision tree (see [supervised-learning.md](../02-classical-ml/supervised-learning.md)), where you can literally read off the exact logic used.

**Why it's hard.** There's active, genuinely promising research (mechanistic interpretability approaches that attempt to reverse-engineer specific learned circuits or features within a network), but this work has so far illuminated meaningful pieces of how specific narrow behaviors arise rather than providing a comprehensive, reliable account of frontier model behavior as a whole. This matters beyond pure scientific curiosity: better interpretability would directly help with debugging unwanted behaviors, verifying safety properties, and building justified trust in high-stakes deployments — which is exactly why its current limitations are a genuinely significant open problem, not just an academic curiosity.

**Status (2025-26).** Real progress, real recognition, and clearly stated limits. In 2025 Anthropic published attribution graphs ("circuit tracing") on Claude 3.5 Haiku: the model is approximated by a *replacement model* — a cross-layer transcoder, which re-expresses the activity of the model's MLP layers in terms of a large set of sparser, more human-interpretable features — and the graph traces which features influence which on a specific prompt. The tooling was open-sourced. MIT Technology Review listed mechanistic interpretability among its 10 Breakthrough Technologies of 2026. The key caveat, stated by the researchers themselves: the replacement model is only *partially faithful* to the real model, so a traced circuit is an explanation of an approximation, and the parts the replacement fails to capture are unexplained. Scaling these methods to give a comprehensive account of a frontier model's behavior remains open.

## Long-horizon agentic reliability

**The problem.** As covered in [timeline-2023-present.md](../08-history/timeline-2023-present.md), models are increasingly trained and used for longer, multi-step, tool-using tasks with reduced step-by-step human supervision. Reliability over long task horizons remains a genuine, unresolved challenge — small per-step error rates compound over many steps, and a model may fail to notice or recover from its own earlier mistakes partway through a long task, an error-propagation problem that gets structurally worse as task length and step count grow, not better.

**Why it's hard.** This isn't simply a matter of "make each individual step more accurate" — even a model with a very low per-step error rate can have a high overall failure rate across a sufficiently long task, purely from compounding, unless the model also reliably self-corrects (notices something has gone wrong and adjusts) rather than continuing to build on a flawed intermediate state. Reliable self-correction over long horizons is itself an open research question, not a solved capability.

**Status (2025-26).** Agentic RL — training the model by letting it act over many steps in code sandboxes and tool environments and rewarding the outcome — became standard practice, and building these "RL environments" became a major investment area (see [rl-for-llms.md](../07-reinforcement-learning/rl-for-llms.md)). Capability on agentic tasks improved visibly, but reliability remains uneven: the compounding-error structure above hasn't changed, and RL adds its own long-known risk that the agent learns to exploit weaknesses in the environment's outcome checker rather than doing the intended task (sometimes called specification gaming or reward hacking). Reliable long-horizon self-correction remains open as of November 2026.

## Energy/compute cost trajectory

**The problem.** Training and serving frontier-scale models requires enormous amounts of compute and, correspondingly, energy — a cost trajectory that has grown substantially as model scale has grown, raising genuine questions about long-term sustainability, resource allocation, and who can realistically afford to train and deploy frontier-scale systems.

**Why it's hard.** This isn't purely a technical problem solvable by a single algorithmic trick — it involves a genuine tension between the scaling-law-driven benefits of larger models/more data/more compute (see [transformer-architecture.md](../03-deep-learning-architectures/transformer-architecture.md)) and real-world resource constraints (energy availability, hardware manufacturing capacity, environmental impact, and cost). The inference-optimization techniques in [06-inference-optimization](../06-inference-optimization/) meaningfully reduce cost-per-token, and this has been genuine, real progress, but they haven't eliminated the underlying tension between "bigger/more-trained models tend to perform better" and "bigger/more-trained models cost more to build and run" — this remains an active, structurally unresolved tension rather than a solved allocation problem.

**Status (2025-26).** Per-token efficiency improved substantially: FP8 and then 4-bit (MXFP4/NVFP4) numerics, ultra-sparse MoE with small active-parameter fractions, and sparse/compressed attention — DeepSeek-V4, for example, reports about 27% of the per-token FLOPs and about 10% of the KV cache of its predecessor at long context (reported). At the same time, demand per task grew: reasoning models spend many "thinking" tokens per answer, test-time compute became an established scaling axis (see [long-term-outlook.md](long-term-outlook.md)), and agents run for many steps. Cheaper tokens have so far been spent on more tokens, so the efficiency gains have not resolved the underlying tension — they have moved it from "cost per token" toward "cost per completed task."

## Evaluation validity

**The problem.** As models have become more capable, it's become genuinely harder to evaluate them meaningfully — many popular benchmarks have become saturated (models score near-perfectly, leaving little room to measure further improvement) or are suspected to have leaked into training data (a model may score well on a benchmark partly because it has effectively memorized answers related to that benchmark from its training data, rather than demonstrating the underlying capability the benchmark was designed to measure).

**Why it's hard.** Designing evaluations that reliably measure genuine capability (rather than benchmark-specific memorization or narrow overfitting to a test's particular format) — especially for open-ended, real-world-relevant tasks rather than narrow, easily-scored ones — is itself a substantial, unsolved methodological challenge, and the field's own tools for measuring progress are consequently less trustworthy than they ideally would be. This is a somewhat self-referential problem worth flagging plainly: this repository's own claims about "current state of the art" throughout its files are only as reliable as the evaluation methodology behind the results being cited, which is itself part of what's unsettled here.

**Status (2025-26).** The pressure increased. Frontier labs now release new models roughly every few months each (for example Gemini 3 Pro, Claude Opus 4.5 and GPT-5.1 in November 2025, followed in 2026 by Gemini 3.1 Pro, GPT-5.5 and the Claude 5 family, per reports), so a new benchmark can go from "hard" to "saturated" within a few release cycles, and the longer a benchmark sits on the public web, the greater the risk it leaks into training data. One partial response is evaluating on freshly written problems that cannot have been in training data — the reported IMO 2025 gold-medal-level results are an example — but such events are rare, narrow and expensive to run. Agentic and long-horizon evaluations, which matter most for current training directions, are also the hardest to make reproducible and cheap. And evaluation validity now carries more weight than before, because pre-release capability evaluations increasingly determine *how* a model is released (see the next problem).

## Dual-use capability and release gating

**The problem.** Some capabilities are inherently dual-use: a model that is good at finding and exploiting software vulnerabilities is useful to defenders patching systems and to attackers breaking into them, and the capability can't easily be built for one group without also becoming available to the other. The question is how to release such models responsibly — who gets access, when, and on what evidence.

**Why it's hard.** Release decisions depend on measuring dangerous capability *before* release, which inherits every problem in the evaluation-validity section above, plus the fact that capability can be unlocked after release by better prompting, tool scaffolding or fine-tuning. Restricting access also delays defenders, not only attackers. Open-weight releases cannot be recalled once published. And there is no agreed, technically grounded definition of where the capability thresholds lie.

**Status (2025-26).** In 2026, frontier labs began gating or staging releases of their most capable models because of offensive-cyber capability — for example, giving initial access only to vetted cyber defenders; this was reported for both Google and Anthropic models. A US Executive Order on frontier AI and cybersecurity was issued on 2 June 2026 (reported). Staged release is therefore now a practice, but how to set thresholds, verify them, and decide when broader release is safe remains open. See [near-term-outlook.md](near-term-outlook.md) for this repository's `[Projection]` on where the practice is heading.
